Bow-tie questions on the NCLEX

Question types · published 2026-08-03 · last updated 2026-08-03 · clinical review evidence pending

A bow-tie item is the format that most rewards structured thinking. It gives you one scenario and asks you to commit to a whole clinical decision at once: what is going on, what you will do about it, and what you will watch to know whether it worked.

The shape of it

Five boxes arranged as a bow-tie:

  • Center — the condition or problem the client is most likely experiencing.
  • Left wing (two boxes) — the actions you would take.
  • Right wing (two boxes) — the parameters you would monitor.

You drag options from a word bank into each box. Everything is drawn from the same pool, which is why plausible-but-wrong options are always available.

Settle the center first, always. Both wings depend on it. If you place actions before you have committed to the condition, you will pick things that are individually reasonable but do not belong to the same clinical picture — and a bow-tie scored as a whole gives you nothing for that.

The order that works

  1. Read the scenario twice before touching anything. The first read gives you the story; the second lets you spot the findings that discriminate between two candidate conditions.
  2. Name the condition in your own words before you look at the word bank. If you read the options first, they will anchor you.
  3. Match your phrase to a center option. If nothing matches, your read is wrong — go back rather than forcing the closest word.
  4. Ask what this condition demands right now. Those are your actions. Prefer what addresses the cause or protects airway, breathing and circulation over what merely documents or reassesses.
  5. Ask what would change first if the action worked — or first if the client deteriorated. Those are your monitoring parameters.

Where candidates lose marks

TrapWhat it looks like
Right action, wrong conditionAn intervention that is genuinely good nursing but belongs to a different problem than the one in the center box.
Monitoring what will not changeChoosing a parameter that is real but too slow or too indirect to tell you whether this action worked.
Two versions of one actionFilling both left boxes with the same idea worded differently, leaving a genuinely required action unplaced.
Assessment when the client is unstableReassessing is safe on paper, but when the scenario has already given you the finding, the exam wants the action it demands.

A worked pattern

A post-operative client is restless, with rising respiratory rate, falling oxygen saturation and diminished breath sounds at one base. The temptation is to reach for options about oxygen and vital signs immediately.

Instead: name it first. Restlessness plus hypoxaemia plus unilateral diminished sounds points to a respiratory problem in that lung, not to pain or anxiety. With the center settled, the left wing wants what addresses oxygenation and lung expansion, and the right wing wants the parameters that move earliest — oxygen saturation, respiratory rate and work of breathing — rather than something that would take hours to shift.

Notice that restlessness is doing real work here. Early hypoxaemia often presents as agitation before anything more dramatic, and exam scenarios use it deliberately as the cue you are supposed to catch.

Common questions

What is a bow-tie question on the NCLEX?

A Next Generation item type with five boxes: the client's most likely condition in the center, two actions on the left, and two parameters to monitor on the right. Options are dragged from a shared word bank.

How is a bow-tie question scored?

As a single item that can carry partial credit, depending on the scoring rule applied. Because the wings depend on the center being right, an incorrect condition usually costs you the whole item rather than one box.

What should I fill in first on a bow-tie?

The center. Both wings are only correct in relation to the condition, so committing to actions before you have identified the problem leads to answers that are individually plausible but clinically inconsistent.
